THRILLER BEAT SHEET — Save the Cat Adapted
==========================================

OPENING IMAGE (p. 1-2)
The world as it is before the threat enters. Often deceptively calm or mundanely ordinary — the quiet before the storm. Establish the protagonist in their controlled, routine life. The image should contrast sharply with the Final Image.

THEME STATED (p. 5)
A hint at the central truth the protagonist must confront — often framed as a warning they dismiss. In thrillers: usually about trust, paranoia, control, or the price of knowing the truth. Someone tells them something they don't believe yet.

SET-UP (p. 1-10)
Establish the protagonist's world, their competence (so we believe in them), their blind spot (so we fear for them), and the ordinary stakes of their life. Plant the elements that will pay off later. Introduce the world that will be disrupted.

CATALYST (p. 12)
The inciting incident that sets the threat in motion. A murder, a discovery, a phone call, a file they weren't supposed to find. The protagonist's life changes irrevocably. They don't know how bad it will get yet.

DEBATE (p. 12-25)
Should they investigate? Report it? Run? The protagonist has a reason to stay out of it and a reason they can't. In thrillers this is often the window of safety — they're not yet in danger, but they're about to step into it. Raising the stakes of their choice.

BREAK INTO TWO (p. 25)
They commit. They cross the line from observer into participant. They report the body, open the file, make the call, ask the question that can't be unasked. The thriller engine ignites.

B STORY (p. 30)
Often a relationship — an ally, a potential love interest, a mentor — who represents a safe harbor and also a point of vulnerability. The antagonist will use this relationship against the protagonist. The B Story character often doesn't know the danger they're in.

FUN AND GAMES (p. 30-55)
The "promise of the premise" — the protagonist investigates, survives close calls, uncovers clues, and believes they're making progress. The audience gets what they came for: the cat-and-mouse, the procedural tension, the chase. The protagonist is often AHEAD of the threat here, or thinks they are.

MIDPOINT (p. 55)
A false victory or a major revelation that reframes everything. Either the protagonist thinks they've solved it (false victory, disaster imminent) or they discover the threat is far larger/closer/more personal than they thought. Stakes double. The real game begins. Often the first direct confrontation.

BAD GUYS CLOSE IN (p. 55-75)
The antagonist goes on offense. The protagonist's allies are compromised, their resources cut off, their safe spaces invaded. Each move to escape results in a tighter trap. The protagonist realizes they've been manipulated — the investigation was anticipated. The enemy knew they were coming.

ALL IS LOST (p. 75)
Everything collapses. The protagonist is caught, exposed, betrayed, or stripped of their last advantage. Someone they trusted is revealed as the enemy, OR someone they care about is in mortal danger, OR they discover the full scope of what they're facing and it seems unwinnable. Death (literal or figurative) looms.

DARK NIGHT OF THE SOUL (p. 75-85)
Alone, trapped, or broken. The protagonist has nothing left — no allies, no plan, no time. This is the moment of reckoning: what do they actually know? What do they actually have? Often the answer is something small and overlooked from the Set-Up. The solution was there all along.

BREAK INTO THREE (p. 85)
The protagonist synthesizes what they know into a final plan. It requires using everything — the skills, the knowledge, the relationships — in a new way. They go on offense. Usually involves a specific location, a specific time, and a specific trap of their own.

FINALE (p. 85-110)
The final confrontation. In thriller: the protagonist must be clever, not just brave — they win through intelligence, preparation, or knowledge the antagonist doesn't expect. The plan nearly fails. The cost is real. The truth is fully revealed. The villain is exposed/stopped/destroyed.

FINAL IMAGE (p. 110)
The world as it is after the threat is gone. Contrast with Opening Image. The protagonist has changed — they are more cautious, more knowing, or more committed to some truth. The ordinary world never looks quite the same again.

THRILLER-SPECIFIC BEAT NOTES:
- The Midpoint revelation should reframe a scene from Act One
- The All Is Lost should involve a betrayal or the death of someone close
- The Break Into Three solution should use something planted in the Set-Up
- The Finale should have at least one more reversal before the final resolution
