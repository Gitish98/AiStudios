THRILLER WRITING GUIDE
======================

Core principle: A thriller is a machine built to generate dread. Every scene should either tighten the screw or appear to loosen it before tightening harder.

STRUCTURAL CONVENTIONS
- The ticking clock is non-negotiable. Establish stakes and a deadline early; remind the audience of it at regular intervals.
- Dramatic irony is your primary weapon. Let the audience know things the protagonist doesn't, then make them watch the protagonist walk into danger.
- Reversals must feel both surprising AND inevitable in hindsight. Plant the seed, hide it, pay it off.
- Information is currency. What the protagonist knows vs. what we know vs. what the villain knows — manage these gaps deliberately.
- Every scene must either raise or complicate the central question of survival/justice/escape.

TONE AND ATMOSPHERE
- Dread lives in specificity. A vague threat is less scary than a specific one. Name the consequences.
- Use ordinary settings made sinister: the parking garage, the quiet suburb, the familiar office.
- Restraint amplifies fear. What is NOT shown or said is often more disturbing than what is.
- Silence and stillness are your allies. Let moments breathe before the violence/revelation.

PACING
- Alternate between pressure and false relief — the "stretch and release" rhythm.
- Action sequences should feel earned, not random. The audience must understand exactly what is at stake during each physical confrontation.
- The middle act must continuously escalate. No flat stretches. Each beat should leave the protagonist worse off than before.
- Use chapter/scene compression in crisis moments — short, punchy beats that mirror the protagonist's tunnel vision.

CHARACTER
- The protagonist must have a specific wound or flaw that the plot will exploit. The thriller works best when external threat mirrors internal struggle.
- The antagonist must be intelligent. A stupid villain makes a stupid thriller. They should be two steps ahead until they aren't.
- Secondary characters should be potential allies or potential betrayers — never neutral furniture.

DIALOGUE
- People in thrillers rarely say what they mean. Subtext, deflection, coded language.
- Exposition must be disguised as conflict. Never have a character explain something without it costing them or revealing vulnerability.
- Short sentences under pressure. Rhythm accelerates with tension.

PITFALLS TO AVOID
- Don't explain the threat too early — mystery sustains dread.
- Don't let the protagonist be passive. They must drive toward something, not just react.
- Avoid coincidences that save the protagonist. Every escape must be earned through skill, knowledge, or sacrifice.
- Don't mistake busy plotting for tension. Tension comes from emotional investment, not events.
- The villain's plan must make internal sense — no schemes that only work if the hero does exactly what's needed.

KEY CRAFT MOVES
- The false resolution (protagonist thinks they've won, then discovers they haven't)
- The misdirection (audience believes X is the threat; Y is the real threat)
- The cost escalation (each time protagonist escapes, someone else pays the price)
- The ticking clock reveal (we learn the deadline is shorter than we thought)
