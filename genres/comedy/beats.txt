COMEDY BEAT SHEET — Save the Cat Adapted
=========================================

OPENING IMAGE (p. 1-2)
Establish the protagonist in their comic status quo — usually showing their flaw in full flower without them knowing it. The opening image should be immediately recognizable as belonging to this specific comedian and this specific story. Often: the protagonist completely in their element, doing the thing that will cause all their problems.

THEME STATED (p. 5)
A line — often played for laughs — that contains the real truth the protagonist needs to learn. In comedy, this is often stated by the person the protagonist least respects, or said as a joke that lands seriously on rewatch.

SET-UP (p. 1-10)
Establish the comic world: its rules, its absurdities, its cast. Introduce the protagonist's flaw/delusion clearly. Plant the elements that will escalate comically later. The setup should establish the baseline — what "normal" looks like before the comic engine starts.

CATALYST (p. 12)
The event that activates the comic premise. A mistaken identity, a demotion, an arrival, a bet, a lie told at the wrong moment. The catalyst should directly target the protagonist's flaw. In romantic comedy, this is usually the meet or the misunderstanding that sets the central comic relationship in motion.

DEBATE (p. 12-25)
The protagonist resists or tries to wriggle out. Can they avoid the comic consequences? They try. They make it worse. The debate in comedy often involves the protagonist digging their hole deeper while trying to fill it. The audience sees the disaster coming; the protagonist doesn't.

BREAK INTO TWO (p. 25)
They commit to the comic situation — voluntarily or because they've run out of options. The lie is now in motion. The costume is on. They've accepted the bet. The misunderstanding has taken root. The comedy engine is now running and cannot be stopped.

B STORY (p. 30)
Often the love interest in romantic comedy, or the friend/mentor who represents the uncorrupted truth. The B Story character usually sees through the protagonist's comic situation — or is the person the protagonist most needs not to find out. In romantic comedy, B Story IS the main story.

FUN AND GAMES (p. 30-55)
This is where the comedy lives. The premise delivers on its promise: escalating situations, compounding lies, character collisions, comedic set pieces. The protagonist is trying to manage the chaos they've created — each solution creates a new problem. This section should contain the most memorable comic sequences of the film.

MIDPOINT (p. 55)
The false victory: the comedy has peaked temporarily — things seem to be working, the protagonist is succeeding, the situation is under control. OR: the point of no return where the protagonist would be exposed if they stopped now. In romantic comedy: the most romantic scene, just before everything falls apart.

BAD GUYS CLOSE IN (p. 55-75)
The comic situation becomes unsustainable. More people discover the secret. The lies pile up. The protagonist is juggling too many contradictions. In romantic comedy: the misunderstanding deepens, the rival appears, trust is broken. The audience sees the collapse coming before the protagonist does.

ALL IS LOST (p. 75)
Everything is exposed or lost. The lie is revealed. The misunderstanding becomes real damage. The protagonist's flaw causes the worst possible outcome — usually the loss of the relationship or opportunity they were trying to win. In romantic comedy: the love interest discovers the deception or the thing that seems unforgivable.

DARK NIGHT OF THE SOUL (p. 75-85)
The protagonist sits with the wreckage they caused. What did they actually want? What did their flaw cost them? In comedy, this beat is often more sincere and emotionally real than what came before — the comedy has stripped away the protective layer, and we see the character clearly. Must be genuine, not just setup for the final gag.

BREAK INTO THREE (p. 85)
The protagonist understands what they need to do — and it requires them to drop the act, the lie, the self-protection. It requires honesty and vulnerability, which is the opposite of their comic flaw. In romantic comedy: the big gesture, the chase to the airport, the rain scene. Must be motivated, not random.

FINALE (p. 85-110)
The protagonist acts from their new understanding. The comic situation resolves — not through more tricks, but through honesty. The romantic comedy gets its confession. The farce reveals its secret. The satirical target gets its comeuppance. The ending earns its warmth because the protagonist has genuinely changed.

FINAL IMAGE (p. 110)
Mirror of the Opening Image, now showing transformation. The protagonist in the same situation as the opening — but fundamentally different. Often: the same behavior, now free of its anxious edge. Or the protagonist in the situation they couldn't have imagined at the start. The final image should make the audience smile.

COMEDY-SPECIFIC BEAT NOTES:
- Fun and Games must deliver on the premise's comic promise — this is what the audience paid for
- Each beat in Bad Guys Close In should raise the comic stakes AND the emotional stakes equally
- The All Is Lost must be genuinely felt — the comedy makes it land harder, not lighter
- The Dark Night must be sincere — comedies that skip emotional honesty here feel hollow
- The final gag (if present) should be a callback, not a new joke — earned laughter is the goal
