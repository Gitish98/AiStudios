ROMANCE WRITING GUIDE
=====================

Core engine: WANT + OBSTACLE + SURRENDER. Romance is the story of two people who belong together, kept apart by external circumstance and internal resistance — until the resistance finally breaks. The audience must feel the connection so strongly that the obstacles are genuinely painful.

THE CENTRAL COUPLE
The connection between the leads must be evident from their first scene together, even if they're fighting. Chemistry on the page comes from:
- Specific, alive dialogue — they speak differently to each other than to anyone else
- Genuine intellectual and emotional engagement — they make each other think
- A power dynamic that shifts (who has the upper hand changes across the story)
- Each seeing something in the other that no one else does

THE OBSTACLES
Romance lives or dies on the quality of its obstacles. There are two kinds:
- External: her fiancé, his terminal diagnosis, the fact that they met during a murder investigation, geographical distance
- Internal: his fear of commitment, her belief that she doesn't deserve love, the lie she told, the wound he's protecting

Both are needed. The external obstacle sets the plot; the internal obstacle makes the story meaningful. The best romance resolves both — the external is cleared just as the internal is overcome.

THE MEET
The first encounter should be:
- Specific (not generic "bumping into someone" — a specific situation that reveals character)
- Charged (something between them from the start, even if framed as antagonism)
- Establishing (we should learn something true about each of them from how they handle the meeting)

ROMANTIC TENSION
Tension is the engine of romance. It is created by:
- Proximity without action (they're close; they don't act; the audience screams)
- Near-misses (the moment they almost say it, almost kiss, almost admit it)
- Misunderstanding (believing the wrong thing about the other, which delays the connection)
- The lie or secret (something withheld that threatens to destroy what's building)
- The rival (a credible third party that forces the protagonist to choose)

THE MOMENT OF TRUTH
Every romance has a scene — the conversation, the confession, the gesture — where the real feelings are finally laid bare. This moment must be:
- Genuinely vulnerable (the character at maximum risk of rejection)
- Specific to these characters (not a generic declaration — these exact words from these exact people)
- Earned (we've seen enough that we believe the feeling is real)

PACING
Romance pacing is about controlling the audience's desire. Give them what they want slowly. Let the tension build. The first touch should feel like it matters. The first kiss, when it comes, should feel like inevitability arriving.

TONE
- Romance allows a wider emotional register than most genres — from heartbreak to joy, sometimes in the same scene.
- Sentimentality is not the enemy; unearned sentimentality is. Make the emotion real and the sentiment earns itself.
- Wit is romance's best friend. Characters who can make each other laugh are characters who belong together.
- Physical specificity matters: what they notice about each other, how proximity affects them, what their body does before their mind catches up.

PITFALLS TO AVOID
- Obstacles that would obviously be resolved by a five-minute conversation (drama from miscommunication frustrates audiences)
- A protagonist who does something unforgivable before the reconciliation (the big romantic gesture can't undo everything)
- Leads who are perfect — characters need flaws that specifically interfere with love
- The B love interest who is clearly inferior (makes the protagonist look stupid for taking so long to choose)
- A climax that is all confession and no action — the protagonist must DO something, not just say something

KEY CRAFT MOVES
- The almost (they nearly say it, nearly touch, nearly kiss — delay creates desire)
- The witness scene (a moment where one of them sees the other being genuinely themselves — authentic, unguarded)
- The false resolution (they get together too soon — something breaks it; the second coming-together is the real climax)
- The rival revelation (the rival turns out to be a catalyst, not a threat — their existence forces the protagonist to choose)
