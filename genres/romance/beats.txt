ROMANCE BEAT SHEET — Save the Cat Adapted
==========================================

OPENING IMAGE (p. 1-2)
The protagonist alone — or in the wrong relationship, the wrong life, the life that's missing something. Establish their current state: what love looks like for them right now, what they believe about love, what wound they carry. The final image will show us what love can look like for them.

THEME STATED (p. 5)
Someone states the romantic truth the protagonist doesn't yet believe. Often framed as cynicism ("Love doesn't last") or false certainty ("I'm not looking for anything serious"). The theme is what they'll have to surrender to be with the right person.

SET-UP (p. 1-10)
Establish the protagonist's world and their relationship to love. Show us the pattern they're stuck in — the type they chase, the walls they've built, the way they self-protect. Introduce the external want (the career, the arrangement, the situation) that will set up the premise. Plant what love will cost them.

CATALYST (p. 12)
The event that puts the two leads in proximity. A new job, an assignment, a move, a bet, a wedding, a mistake. Can also be the meet — the moment the love interest enters the story. The catalyst should make their interaction necessary, not optional. They cannot avoid each other.

DEBATE (p. 12-25)
The protagonist resists. There are good reasons not to pursue this: he's her boss, she's engaged, it would ruin the friendship, one of them is leaving town. The debate shows us exactly what stands between them — both the external obstacle and the hint of internal resistance. They try to keep it professional, platonic, manageable.

BREAK INTO TWO (p. 25)
They commit to the situation that will bring them together — not to each other (yet), but to the proximity. They take the job, accept the dare, agree to help with the wedding. Something shifts: they choose to stay in each other's orbit.

B STORY (p. 30)
Often IS the romance story — the developing relationship is both A and B. Or the B Story is a friend/mentor who represents a different model of love: the one who knows what the protagonist needs, the one in the relationship they think they want but don't, the one whose relationship holds up a mirror.

FUN AND GAMES (p. 30-55)
The falling — the part of falling in love that the audience lives for. Sparring that tips into warmth. Shared secrets. The moment their guard drops. The first almost. Genuine connection building under the surface of whatever premise brought them together. This section should be alive with romantic energy — specific, particular, these two people and no one else.

MIDPOINT (p. 55)
The most intimate scene before the fall. They've gotten close; something real has been shared; the audience believes they're going to make it. Then: a complication arrives (the rival appears, the lie is almost exposed, a choice is demanded). In some romances, this is the first kiss — a false victory that precedes the dark turn.

BAD GUYS CLOSE IN (p. 55-75)
The distance grows. The secret, the lie, the misunderstanding, the rival — something drives a wedge. They may try to pull back to safety. Past wounds resurface. The closer they've gotten, the more vulnerable they are, the worse the hurt when it comes. The audience is rooting desperately.

ALL IS LOST (p. 75)
The worst thing. The lie is exposed. The secret comes out. The protagonist makes the choice that pushes the love interest away. Or the love interest makes the choice that seems to end it. The relationship is broken, the connection severed, and it feels like it can't be repaired. One or both of them withdraws completely.

DARK NIGHT OF THE SOUL (p. 75-85)
The protagonist sits with what they've lost and why. What did they do? What are they afraid of? What does love actually require of them that they've been withholding? This section shouldn't be rushed — the audience needs to feel the weight of the loss, and the protagonist needs to arrive at a real reckoning about who they've been.

BREAK INTO THREE (p. 85)
The protagonist decides to try — knowing the risk, knowing they might be rejected, choosing to be vulnerable anyway. This is the emotional climax of their arc: choosing love over self-protection. They must DO something: go somewhere, say something, make a gesture that costs them their dignity or their safety.

FINALE (p. 85-110)
The protagonist acts. The grand gesture, the confession, the chase to the airport, the scene in the rain. The love interest must choose too — they're not simply accepting the gesture; they're making their own choice. The reconciliation should feel hard-won and real. They come together with full knowledge of who each other is.

FINAL IMAGE (p. 110)
The protagonist no longer alone, or alone in a new way — one that's a choice, not a wound. The image should echo the Opening Image while showing transformation: who they were vs. who they've become through love. The final beat should make the audience feel the warmth of the connection, not just the resolution of the plot.

ROMANCE-SPECIFIC BEAT NOTES:
- Fun and Games is the heart of the film — give it time, give it specificity, let the connection breathe
- The Midpoint moment of intimacy should be the scene audiences quote — make it specific to these characters
- Bad Guys Close In should feel emotionally real, not contrived — we must feel why the obstacle matters
- The All Is Lost must genuinely feel like the end — the audience must believe it might not work out
- The grand gesture in the Finale should be surprising and inevitable — something that only this character could do
