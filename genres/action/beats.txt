ACTION BEAT SHEET — Save the Cat Adapted
=========================================

OPENING IMAGE (p. 1-2)
Often an action sequence or the immediate aftermath of one — or the protagonist in the life they're about to have torn away. Establish physical competence AND vulnerability from the first frame. The opening image should define the protagonist's world: the job, the family, the body, the skill. What will they fight for?

THEME STATED (p. 5)
A line — often spoken during a quiet moment — that names what the protagonist is really fighting for. In action, the theme is usually about protection (who deserves it, what it costs), justice, or identity (what they're willing to do, who they'll become). Often stated by someone who doesn't survive the film.

SET-UP (p. 1-10)
Establish the world, the protagonist's competence, and the personal stakes that will make the action meaningful. Introduce the relationships the protagonist will be fighting to protect. Plant the limitation that will matter later. Establish what the protagonist has to lose — career, family, life. Make it specific and valuable.

CATALYST (p. 12)
The event that starts everything: an attack, an assignment, a discovery, a kidnapping, a death. In action, the catalyst is usually violent or the immediate threat of violence. Something is taken from the protagonist (a person, a job, a life) or a threat is declared. The story's conflict is named.

DEBATE (p. 12-25)
The protagonist tries to solve the problem through legitimate means: authorities, systems, the chain of command. These fail. In action, the debate is often about whether to fight or comply — the forces against the protagonist are too large to fight conventionally. The protagonist is being pushed toward the only option left.

BREAK INTO TWO (p. 25)
The protagonist goes rogue — breaks from the system, takes the assignment that can't be officially sanctioned, pursues the target that everyone says is too dangerous. They step outside the safety of normal operating procedure. From here, they're on their own. The physical journey begins.

B STORY (p. 30)
The relationship the protagonist is ultimately fighting for: a partner, a family member, a hostage, a mentor. The B Story character humanizes the protagonist's mission — they are what the action is ultimately in service of. Often in danger throughout the second act.

FUN AND GAMES (p. 30-55)
The action movie delivers: first major set piece(s), establishing the protagonist's skill and the threat's scale. Reconnaissance, infiltration, first contact with the enemy. The protagonist begins making progress against the threat. The action is dynamic and specific, establishing the physical geography of the story world. The audience should be thrilling to the spectacle.

MIDPOINT (p. 55)
A major victory that turns out to be a trap, OR a revelation that makes the threat far bigger than it appeared. The stakes escalate. The personal and the global intersect: the threat is now both personally and systemically dangerous. Often involves the first direct confrontation between protagonist and primary antagonist, which the protagonist survives but barely.

BAD GUYS CLOSE IN (p. 55-75)
The second act pressure cooker. The protagonist's resources are cut off, their allies are compromised, their safe positions are overrun. Each tactical win costs more than it gains. The villain adapts. The protagonist realizes the threat is inside the system they trusted, or larger than they knew, or personally aimed at them. They're being herded.

ALL IS LOST (p. 75)
Total defeat. The protagonist is captured, the villain has achieved their major objective, the person being protected is in mortal danger. The protagonist's plan has failed, their allies are gone or dead, and the clock is ticking on an outcome that cannot be allowed. This beat should feel genuinely unwinnable.

DARK NIGHT OF THE SOUL (p. 75-85)
Often short in action — the protagonist in a cell, a wreckage, hiding. But it must be real: the protagonist must reckon with what they're up against, what they have left, and what they're willing to do. The answer to the last question is often: whatever it takes, at whatever cost. The line they've been reluctant to cross gets crossed here, in their mind first.

BREAK INTO THREE (p. 85)
The protagonist synthesizes their resources — skills, knowledge, the help of the B Story character — into a final plan. The plan may be desperate and asymmetric (one person against a fortified position) but it has internal logic. They choose to go in. The final act begins.

FINALE (p. 85-110)
The climactic set piece: the assault on the position, the confrontation with the primary antagonist, the rescue, the stopping of the threat. The plan goes wrong; the protagonist must improvise. The wound and the limitation from the setup matter here — the protagonist wins by using them, not despite them. The villain is defeated specifically, not generically. The cost is real.

FINAL IMAGE (p. 110)
The protagonist in the aftermath: the B Story character saved, the threat neutralized, but the protagonist changed by what it took. The final image often shows the protagonist's new relationship to violence, to their mission, to what they're protecting. Action endings should feel earned — the audience should feel the relief and the cost simultaneously.

ACTION-SPECIFIC BEAT NOTES:
- Each act should have at least one major set piece — Plan, execute, adjust under fire
- Fun and Games should establish the physical rules of this action world clearly
- The Midpoint set piece should be the film's most spectacular up to that point, then immediately get worse
- All Is Lost must put the B Story character (the person being protected) in direct, imminent jeopardy
- The Finale set piece should be the protagonist at maximum disadvantage winning through the one thing only they can do
