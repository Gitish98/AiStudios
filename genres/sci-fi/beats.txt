SCI-FI BEAT SHEET — Save the Cat Adapted
=========================================

OPENING IMAGE (p. 1-2)
Establish the world — or the protagonist's place in it. The opening image in sci-fi often does double duty: it must orient the audience in the speculative setting AND show us who the protagonist is in that world. The image should carry the seed of the film's central question. What does this world feel like from the inside?

THEME STATED (p. 5)
Someone articulates the human question at the story's heart — usually without knowing it. In sci-fi, theme statements often come as conversations about the technology, the system, or the world that actually encode the film's philosophical question. "Machines don't have feelings" / "We're already colonists" / "It doesn't matter where we're from."

SET-UP (p. 1-10)
Establish the world through the protagonist's navigation of it. Reveal the rules, the stakes, the society through behavior. Show us what is normal here and what is wrong with it. Plant the elements the story will pay off. Introduce the protagonist's wound or limitation that the speculative premise will exploit.

CATALYST (p. 12)
The event that activates the story's central question. A discovery, an arrival, a malfunction, an assignment, a signal. In sci-fi, the catalyst often involves contact with the speculative element: the AI wakes up, the ship goes off course, the time machine works, the alien is found. Everything changes.

DEBATE (p. 12-25)
The protagonist has a reason to engage with the speculative element and a reason to fear it. The debate often externally mirrors an internal resistance: confronting the AI is confronting consciousness; investigating the alien signal is confronting isolation. Establish the cost of engagement clearly.

BREAK INTO TWO (p. 25)
The protagonist commits to the journey — into the alien ship, into the time stream, into the new world, into the investigation. They cross the threshold of the speculative. There is no clean return. The "what if" is now fully activated and the protagonist is inside it.

B STORY (p. 30)
Often a relationship that represents either the human stakes (what the protagonist risks losing by going further into the speculative) or the speculative stakes (the AI, the alien, the other consciousness that will change the protagonist's understanding of their question). The B Story bridges the human and the speculative.

FUN AND GAMES (p. 30-55)
The film delivers on the premise — this is the part of the movie the audience anticipated. The world is explored, the wonder is shown, the implications of the "what if" are developed. The protagonist engages with the speculative element and both discovers and is changed by it. Set pieces, revelations, world-building moments that reward the audience's investment.

MIDPOINT (p. 55)
A revelation that reframes the central question. The ai isn't just following instructions — it wants something. The alien isn't a threat — it's a warning. The time travel doesn't work the way they thought. The false victory: the protagonist thinks they understand, and they don't. Stakes expand dramatically. The film's second half is now a different story than the first.

BAD GUYS CLOSE IN (p. 55-75)
The speculative element becomes threatening — not because it was always dangerous but because the protagonist now understands what's at stake. The system closes in. The technology has consequences. The alien has an agenda. Every move creates complications. The protagonist's human relationships are strained by what they're learning.

ALL IS LOST (p. 75)
Total collapse. The speculative element exacts its full cost. Someone dies, something is revealed, a choice is made that cannot be undone. The protagonist's world understanding is shattered. In sci-fi, the All Is Lost often involves a revelation that makes everything the protagonist worked for seem wrong or impossible.

DARK NIGHT OF THE SOUL (p. 75-85)
The protagonist faces the human question of the film at maximum personal cost. They must decide: what do they believe? What does consciousness mean? What do we owe? What makes us human? The answer must be personal and specific — not a philosophical position but a gut-level choice made in extremity.

BREAK INTO THREE (p. 85)
The protagonist finds a synthesis — a way to use the speculative element in service of the human truth they've discovered. The technology, the alien, the time machine — it becomes a tool for the protagonist's human choice rather than the other way around. They have an answer, or at least an action.

FINALE (p. 85-110)
The protagonist acts on their understanding. The speculative premise is confronted directly: the AI is given a choice, the alien contact is completed, the time loop is broken. The resolution requires both human courage and engagement with the speculative element. Cost is real. The answer to the central question is demonstrated through action, not speech.

FINAL IMAGE (p. 110)
The world after. The protagonist changed by the encounter with the speculative. The final image often echoes the opening but with new meaning: the same world seen differently, or a new world that reflects what the protagonist has become. Sci-fi endings should feel earned at both the speculative AND the human level.

SCI-FI-SPECIFIC BEAT NOTES:
- World-building should be embedded in every section, not concentrated in the Set-Up
- The Midpoint revelation should reframe the speculative element's meaning, not just escalate its danger
- Fun and Games must include wonder-beats — moments where the strangeness of the world is fully felt
- The All Is Lost often involves a choice between the speculative element and a human relationship
- The Finale resolution should use the speculative element as the vehicle for the human truth
