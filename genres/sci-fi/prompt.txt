SCI-FI WRITING GUIDE
====================

Core engine: THE "WHAT IF" + HUMAN STAKES. Science fiction asks a speculative question — "What if technology could X?", "What if first contact happened?", "What if humanity colonized Y?" — and then answers it with a human story. The speculative element must generate the dramatic situation; the human element must make the audience care.

THE CENTRAL "WHAT IF"
Every science fiction screenplay should be reducible to a single "what if" that is:
- Specific enough to generate clear dramatic consequences
- Novel enough to justify the setting
- Connected to something real about the present world

The "what if" is your premise engine. Every major plot development should flow from its logic.

WORLD-BUILDING ON THE PAGE
- Establish the world through behavior, not exposition. Characters who live in this world don't explain it — they navigate it.
- Introduce world-defining details through action and conflict, not information dumps
- Three well-chosen specific details create a world more convincingly than three paragraphs of background
- The world should feel like it has a history beyond what we see — implied depth rather than stated depth

THE INTERNAL LOGIC
Science fiction lives or dies on internal consistency. Establish your rules clearly (even if obliquely) and adhere to them. Audiences are forgiving of physical impossibility but unforgiving of rule-breaking. If your faster-than-light drive has a limitation, that limitation must matter to the plot.

THE TECHNOLOGY-HUMANITY MIRROR
The best sci-fi uses its speculative element to examine something true about human experience:
- Artificial intelligence: what makes us conscious? what do we owe to created minds?
- Time travel: regret, fate, the possibility of redemption
- Alien contact: what does "other" mean? what does encounter do to identity?
- Genetic modification: the nature of self, the tyranny of perfection
- Dystopian systems: power, freedom, complicity, the individual vs. the state

Your speculative element should be a mirror, not just a backdrop.

PACING
- Sci-fi has a higher exposition budget than most genres — audiences will tolerate more information because they want to understand the world.
- But exposition must be dramatized. The best sci-fi exposition is delivered under pressure, during conflict, or through character behavior.
- Balance wonder-beats (the big visual/conceptual moments) with intimate human scenes. The intimate scenes make the wonder meaningful.

VISUAL LANGUAGE
Science fiction is a visual genre. Think in images:
- The establishing shots of the world (what does this future/past/alternate reality look like?)
- The technology in use (what does it do to the human body? how does it feel? what are its textures?)
- The alien/unfamiliar element made physically specific

PITFALLS TO AVOID
- World-building that crowds out character (we understand the world but don't care about anyone in it)
- Technology that solves problems rather than creating them (every sci-fi element should generate drama, not resolve it)
- The chosen-one protagonist with no specific inner life beyond their special destiny
- Alien civilizations that are just humans with different makeup
- Deus ex machina technology that appears in Act Three to solve the problem

THE HUMAN QUESTION
Every major sci-fi film has a human question at its heart:
- Who am I? (identity, consciousness)
- What do I owe? (responsibility, connection)
- What have we become? (civilization, progress)
- What makes us human? (essence, technology)

Name your human question. Make it the protagonist's personal journey.

KEY CRAFT MOVES
- The wonder beat (a scene whose only purpose is to make the audience feel the scale or strangeness of this world)
- The technology-as-metaphor scene (where the speculative element directly externalizes the protagonist's internal struggle)
- The first contact (meeting the alien/AI/future/other for the first time — must be specific, not generic)
- The cost of the premise (the speculative element extracts a price — what is it, and who pays?)
