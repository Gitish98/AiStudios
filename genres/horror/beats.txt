HORROR BEAT SHEET — Save the Cat Adapted
=========================================

OPENING IMAGE (p. 1-2)
The world before the horror enters — often deceptively safe, warm, or ordinary. Establishes exactly what the protagonist stands to lose. May include a subtle wrong note that attentive viewers will catch on rewatch. Contrast with the Final Image is essential.

THEME STATED (p. 5)
A comment or moment that names the horror's deeper meaning — often without anyone knowing it. The theme in horror is usually about denial: refusing to believe what you see, refusing to acknowledge what's wrong, the cost of ignoring warning signs.

SET-UP (p. 1-10)
Establish the protagonist's world with enough warmth and specificity that the audience invests in it. Show us what they love, what they fear losing, what they are blind to. Plant the elements the horror will weaponize. The set-up should feel like ordinary life — not horror-movie setup.

CATALYST (p. 12)
The moment the horror enters the world. Can be: arrival at the location, discovery of the wrong thing, a visitor, a noise, an absence. The protagonist may not understand its significance yet. The audience should sense it. The door has opened.

DEBATE (p. 12-25)
The protagonist's rational mind resists. They explain away the evidence. They try to reassure themselves and each other. This is not stupidity — this is human. The debate is between their desire for normalcy and the evidence they can't quite dismiss. Someone (usually) believes before the protagonist does.

BREAK INTO TWO (p. 25)
The protagonist commits to investigating, staying, or fighting — even when leaving would be safer. They have a reason: love, duty, money, disbelief. This is the point where the horror begins in earnest. The threat is now engaged.

B STORY (p. 30)
Often a relationship under pressure — a couple, a parent and child, siblings. The horror will use this relationship as leverage. The B Story character may be the first to show signs of the threat's influence, or the one whose safety keeps the protagonist from leaving.

FUN AND GAMES (p. 30-55)
The "horror movie" portion of the horror movie: the protagonist explores the threat, gets glimpses, has close calls, discovers the rules of what they're dealing with. Each encounter should escalate. The protagonist is reactive here — they're trying to understand while the threat hunts them.

MIDPOINT (p. 55)
The first direct, undeniable confrontation with the full nature of the threat. Nothing can be explained away after this. Stakes escalate massively. Often includes a death or near-death, or a revelation that makes the situation far worse than assumed. The protagonist must now act rather than investigate.

BAD GUYS CLOSE IN (p. 55-75)
The threat becomes relentless. Safe spaces are compromised. Allies are lost, turned, or killed. Each escape route closes. The protagonist is running out of options and the horror is closing in on what matters most. The threat may be revealed to be more personal, more inescapable, than they thought.

ALL IS LOST (p. 75)
Total collapse. The worst thing happens. The person they were protecting is lost, the escape route is gone, they are caught, cornered, or transformed. The protagonist hits physical and emotional bottom. Death (literal or symbolic) is imminent. This beat should genuinely feel like the end.

DARK NIGHT OF THE SOUL (p. 75-85)
Alone, possibly injured, possibly contaminated. The protagonist must decide: fight or succumb. This is the moment where the theme clicks — the thing they were denying, the truth they wouldn't face. Horror protagonists who survive often do so by accepting something terrible about their situation — or themselves.

BREAK INTO THREE (p. 85)
The protagonist finds their weapon — physical, psychological, or both. Synthesizes everything they've learned about the threat. Makes a plan that uses the rules they've discovered. Accepts the cost. Stops running and turns to fight.

FINALE (p. 85-110)
The final confrontation with the horror. Plans go wrong. The cost is real. The protagonist wins, escapes, or — in some horror — doesn't. If they win, it should feel hard-won, not easy. If they escape, something has changed permanently. The threat may be destroyed but cannot be unseen.

FINAL IMAGE (p. 110)
Contrast with the Opening Image. The protagonist has survived — but the world doesn't look the same. Safety is now provisional. Normalcy is scarred. Some horror ends with a final sting: the threat wasn't fully defeated, the protagonist carries something with them. Earned ambiguity, not cheap sequel-bait.

HORROR-SPECIFIC BEAT NOTES:
- The Debate must show credible reasons to stay — not stupidity
- The Fun and Games escalates in clearly defined stages: glimpse → evidence → encounter → confrontation
- The All Is Lost should involve the horror doing the worst thing you can imagine for this specific story
- The Dark Night is often the protagonist's most vulnerable moment — consider giving them one true ally here, just long enough to lose them
- The Finale must cost something real; a perfect clean resolution can undermine the horror's reality
