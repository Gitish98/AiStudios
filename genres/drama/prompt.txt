DRAMA WRITING GUIDE
===================

Core principle: Drama is the art of making people feel the full weight of being human. It earns tears not through manipulation but through truth — characters we recognize, situations we understand, costs that feel real.

STRUCTURAL CONVENTIONS
- Drama is driven by internal conflict externalized. The protagonist's inner wound or flaw must be the engine of the story, not just a backstory detail.
- The three-act structure in drama is about transformation: who the protagonist is vs. who they need to become vs. whether they actually get there.
- Drama can be quiet. Big events don't require big scenes. A look across a room can carry more weight than an explosion.
- Every relationship in the script should have history, power dynamics, and unspoken things. Scenes are conversations on top of conversations.
- The ending doesn't need to be happy, but it must feel true. Audiences forgive tragedy; they don't forgive falseness.

TONE AND ATMOSPHERE
- Specificity creates universality. Write specific people in specific places doing specific things — audiences recognize the general in the particular.
- Restraint is a dramatic tool. The moment that almost breaks someone open is often more powerful than the moment that does.
- The physical world carries emotional weight. How a character inhabits their space, what they touch, what they avoid — all meaning.
- Silence between characters is dialogue. What goes unsaid in drama is often the most important thing.

PACING
- Drama breathes. Don't rush the moments that need to sit.
- Scene length should match emotional weight — the more important the beat, the more time it deserves.
- Counterpoint scenes (lighter moments amid grief, humor amid despair) are essential. They make the dark moments darker by contrast.
- Rising tension in drama is relational: the closer we get to the truth, the harder it becomes to avoid.

CHARACTER
- Every character has a version of the truth they tell themselves. Drama often reveals that version to be incomplete or false.
- The want (external goal) and need (internal truth) must be clearly distinct and in tension. The character pursues the want and arrives at the need.
- Secondary characters should reflect, contrast, or challenge the protagonist's worldview — not just serve the plot.
- Characters should surprise us while still making sense. Real people are contradictory.

DIALOGUE
- Dramatic dialogue lives in what people don't say. The subtext is the text.
- People in drama talk about one thing while meaning another. Arguments about dishes are arguments about love. Conversations about money are conversations about worth.
- Rhythm matters: a character's syntax, vocabulary, and sentence structure reveals who they are.
- Monologues must be earned — they're moments when the pressure gets so great that the subtext finally breaks through.

THEMATIC WORK
- Theme should emerge from the character's choices, not from speeches. Show don't tell — but for emotional truth, not just plot.
- Drama works best when the thematic question is genuinely difficult. There should be no easy answer.
- The story should complicate its own argument. The counterpoint to the theme should be articulated honestly.

PITFALLS TO AVOID
- Don't mistake suffering for drama. Characters must be active — even when what they're doing is enduring. What do they want? Why? What does it cost?
- Avoid on-the-nose dialogue. If a character says exactly what they feel, the scene has failed.
- Don't protect your characters. Let the situation cost them something real.
- Avoid undercutting dramatic moments with irony or humor as a deflection device.
- The ending must be earned — avoid epiphanies that aren't set up, or resolutions that cost nothing.

KEY CRAFT MOVES
- The silent scene (two characters in the same space, nothing said — everything felt)
- The displacement activity (characters do something physical while the real conversation happens around it)
- The too-late realization (protagonist understands what they needed to do after the moment has passed)
- The witness (a secondary character who sees what the protagonist can't see about themselves)
