DRAMA BEAT SHEET — Save the Cat Adapted
========================================

OPENING IMAGE (p. 1-2)
A precise, specific image of the protagonist in their current state — often showing the wound or limitation they carry without naming it. The world they've constructed around their unacknowledged need. Should contrast with where they end up.

THEME STATED (p. 5)
Someone — often not the protagonist — articulates what the story is really about. Usually disguised as advice, a question, or a throwaway comment. The protagonist dismisses it or doesn't fully hear it. The audience should feel it land even when the character doesn't.

SET-UP (p. 1-10)
Establish the protagonist's world with ruthless economy. Their relationships, their routine, their wound. Plant what the drama will pay off. Introduce the tension that already exists beneath the surface — drama stories often begin when something is already about to crack.

CATALYST (p. 12)
An external event that forces the protagonist into motion. In drama: often a loss, an arrival, a diagnosis, a return, or a discovery that makes the status quo impossible. Not necessarily violent — it can be as quiet as a letter, a phone call, or a person walking through a door.

DEBATE (p. 12-25)
The protagonist resists the change the catalyst demands. They try to maintain the life they've built. In drama, this resistance is emotionally grounded — we understand why they don't want to change even as we see they must. This section reveals the depth of the wound.

BREAK INTO TWO (p. 25)
The protagonist chooses to engage with the situation rather than avoid it. They make a commitment — even if reluctant. In drama, this is often a relational commitment: to help someone, to confront someone, to return somewhere.

B STORY (p. 30)
The relationship that will carry the thematic weight of the story. In drama, this is often a new relationship, a healing old one, or a deteriorating one that forces the protagonist to reckon with who they are. The B Story character holds up a mirror.

FUN AND GAMES (p. 30-55)
The "upside" of the new situation — before the cost comes due. The protagonist is engaging, making progress, perhaps experiencing hope. In drama: genuine connection, a sense that change might be possible. This section should make the audience want things to work out.

MIDPOINT (p. 55)
Either a false victory (things seem to be working, which precedes a harder fall) or a public moment of truth where the protagonist's inner struggle is exposed. In drama, the midpoint is often the scene where the protagonist comes closest to becoming who they need to be — and pulls back at the last moment.

BAD GUYS CLOSE IN (p. 55-75)
The cost of change begins to mount. Relationships strain, old patterns re-assert, the wound is prodded. In drama, "bad guys" are often internal forces — fear, shame, habit, self-deception — as much as external circumstances. The protagonist's flaw actively undermines what they're trying to build.

ALL IS LOST (p. 75)
The relationship or situation that matters most collapses. The protagonist's worst fear is realized or their greatest failure is enacted. Someone is hurt. Something is broken that may not be fixable. In drama, this is often the protagonist's fault — their flaw costs them exactly what they needed.

DARK NIGHT OF THE SOUL (p. 75-85)
The protagonist sits with what they've done and who they are. No action — just reckoning. In drama, this is often the most important scene in the script. The protagonist must find, within themselves, the will to be different — not because it's easy, but because they finally understand the cost of not changing.

BREAK INTO THREE (p. 85)
The protagonist integrates what they've learned — the theme finally takes hold. They understand what they need to do, even knowing it will be hard or painful. Often involves reaching out, returning, or making a gesture that requires vulnerability.

FINALE (p. 85-110)
The protagonist acts from their new understanding. Relationships are confronted, truths are spoken, choices are made. In drama, the resolution isn't always triumphant — sometimes the protagonist succeeds in understanding but the damage is done. The ending feels true, not easy.

FINAL IMAGE (p. 110)
Mirror of the Opening Image, showing change — or the precise nature of what didn't change. In drama, the final image often carries the emotional resolution even when words can't. What the protagonist does in the last moment tells us everything about who they've become.

DRAMA-SPECIFIC BEAT NOTES:
- The Debate section should show us the pain of the status quo — change is hard, but so is staying
- The Fun and Games should give us genuine hope — otherwise we won't feel the fall
- The All Is Lost must be the protagonist's fault, at least partially — otherwise they have no agency
- The Dark Night should be quiet — resist the urge to dramatize it with plot events
- The Finale should contain at least one scene of genuine honesty between characters
